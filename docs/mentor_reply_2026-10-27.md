**1. What C1 through C24 are.** They are my own numbering, not a standard from the field. Each one is a pre-registered success criterion: a metric, a baseline, a threshold and the data it is scored on, written to a file (`results/success_criteria*.csv`) and committed *before* the notebook that scores it runs. Failures are reported as registered and never re-scored. In groups:

- C1–C5: the queue completion model against cohort averages at entry — Uno's C and the Brier score at 3, 5 and 7 years (pass if the 95% paired bootstrap interval of the difference excludes zero on the better side), a calibration slope between 0.9 and 1.1, and a top-decile lift of at least 1.5. 8 of 12 lines pass; the slope fails at 3 and 5 years.
- C6–C12: the model against the *lookup* a careful analyst would build by hand (the share built among earlier requests of the same region, technology and size), at utility scale; the landmark refit two years in; a backtest of a model-managed book; and a leakage guard. 3 of 12 pass: the lookup ties the model at entry, the landmark model wins once the agreement is dated, and no leakage is found.
- C13–C19: the same tests on MISO and SPP with the 2026 file, the milestone-managed book, and other learners against LightGBM. 12 of 30 pass.
- C20: the machine-learning forecasts of lease output, plant capacity factors and the three prices against the best of five transparent baselines (last value, seasonal naive, Arps decline, technology-month median, random walk), by the paired moving-block bootstrap interval of the error difference. 3 of 12 pass, all for plant output.
- C21–C24: the four books under one budget — risk-aware against NPV ranking and equal capital on expected NPV minus the worst-5% average loss, stability under six stresses, and the tail against a single-sector book. 2 of 4 pass as registered.

In all, 28 of the 70 lines registered at proposal time pass; the later challenger tests added C25–C41 with the same discipline. The report defines every one: Appendix A carries the grouped record (Tables A1–A3), the ledger `docs/registration_ledger.md` lists all 78 lines of C1–C32 with the verdict as first scored and any correction made afterwards, and each criteria file holds the metric, the baseline and the threshold line by line. **3. The data sources, how they are used, and the cleaning.** They are not joined into one table. They feed three panels, each at its own grain, and meet only at the candidate level:

- The queue panel: the LBNL *Queued Up* project file (one row per interconnection request), with the ISO queue files and EIA-860M used to date milestones and operations. It trains the completion-and-timing models.
- The energy panel: BSEE OGOR-A lease-month production, EIA-923 plant × prime-mover-month generation for Texas, and the market months (WTI, Henry Hub, ERCOT hub prices). Each series stays at its reported grain; EIA-923 is never split to generators, and BSEE is joined by lease and month with one-to-many audits.
- The cost and emissions layer: NREL ATB 2024 and EPA eGRID, read as parameters rather than joined.

The forty candidates — twenty Gulf leases, twenty ERCOT requests — are where the panels meet: each candidate's cash flows draw its output from the lease or plant forecasts, its prices from the joint scenario paths, its completion chance and operation year from the queue models, and its costs from ATB or the documented lease grid.

The cleaning is written as numbered rules, each a function that logs how many rows it touched. For the queue file, R1–R9: convert Excel serial dates; treat 1970-01-01 as missing; keep operation and withdrawal dates only where they can be real; keep generation requests; drop non-positive capacities and flag very large ones; build the project key and resolve duplicates; drop sparse fields and give missing categories their own level; flag hybrids. For the energy panel, R10–R13: no duplicate lease-month or plant-month keys (the joins are one-to-many by construction); negative volumes removed and zero-production months flagged; negative plant generation handled for batteries; missing months in each market series counted. On top of that, every feature is lagged by its source's release lag (about two months for EIA-923, three for BSEE, same month for prices), units are standardised (bbl, Mcf, MWh, real 2024 dollars), and a truncation test proves that no feature changes when post-origin data are removed. The data readiness report (`docs/data_readiness.md`) ends in GO before any modelling notebook was written.

**4. What Milestone II contributed, and what changed in the predictive part.** Two different things are involved, and I should have kept them apart in the proposal. My Milestone II project was net-load forecasting on EIA-930 data; the only piece reused from it is the ERCOT price-API client, credited in the statement of work. The predictive models the proposal calls "already built" are this capstone's own first phase: the survival and hazard models of which interconnection requests get built and when, built and registered in the first proposal form's period (C1–C19).

So yes, the goal was to keep that work and move to prescription. The predictive part was kept as registered, and four things were added or changed, each because a review found a problem:

- The comparison that matters became the lookup, not the cohort average. Against cohort averages the model looks strong; at utility scale it ties the lookup at entry, so the claim moved to two years in, where the landmark model reaches Uno's C 0.80 against 0.59.
- The level was corrected. Refitted on what was known in 2020, the model ranked requests better than the lookup (AUC 0.731 against 0.686) but put ERCOT's five-year chance at 0.380 where 0.238 operated; that ratio, knowable on the decision date, corrects the 2025 curves. AUC is not calibration.
- Post-entry information was removed from the main models. Study phase and the agreement date are known only after entry; after the review of 19 October they enter only the labelled landmark model. The candidate set was also rebuilt from what was public on 1 January 2025 (the LBNL 2024 edition and ERCOT's 2024 reports).
- A forward test was added. The 2026 edition records what happened in 2025: of 1,441 live requests, 30 operated; the raw model expected 125, the level-corrected model 78, the lookup 17. Nothing was refitted.

The new forecasts for output and prices (C20) are new work. And the finding that matters for the report is that better prediction did not make a better decision: the book chosen with the model's queue curves is not demonstrably better than the book chosen with the lookup's (+$37M [−$76M, +$151M] on the registered metric). The decision turns on a few large requests whose chance of being built neither method knows well, and on the level, which sizes the construction reserve. That is why the report's argument is about the constraints, the capital plan and the tail rather than about the forecasts.

**5. What Claude does and does not do.** The proposal's Section 5 described an advisor that recommends a book per scenario from the result tables and is scored in the decision gym. That was built and scored: in the gym the advisor trailed the registered rule by $5.1M a situation [−$11.0M, −$1.5M], worse in 12 of 14, and when it explained results, 119 of 119 numbers it cited were in its fact sheet. The review of 19 October then changed the design, and the report's main AI component is a *challenger* rather than an advisor: the model does not choose the book at all. It never predicts and never computes. The boundary is enforced by code, not by the prompt, and it has three parts:

- *Reading.* The model reads the committee's statement into typed rules — tail weight, chance-of-loss and tail limits, exclusions, a liquidity line, budget use — as parameters a validator accepts or rejects; anything it cannot represent it must name and leave unset. The committee sees the reading and confirms it before anything is computed. On the registered test it read 40 of 40 paraphrases exactly.
- *Computing.* Tools, not the model, score the proposal on held-out scenarios — the rules it breaks, the calls it cannot fund, the members that lose in most scenarios — and compute two alternatives under the committee's own rules: the smallest repair and the optimizer's book. The model never writes a value the engine uses.
- *Arguing.* The model writes a memo of at most three vulnerabilities, each tied to numbered facts on a computed fact sheet. Code checks every number in the memo against that sheet and flags any that is not there, and code decides whether the model may "guide" toward another book (only one that keeps every rule and either fixes a breach or beats the proposal with an interval clear of zero). In the worked example in Section 9, the memo cited 35 numbers; 34 were on the sheet and one was flagged.

So the model relays and argues; it does not recommend from its own judgment. Its value was measured against a control with the same tools and no language model (the checklist): on 48 simulated committees the tools added $76M per case over the proposal, the model $43M more [+$9M, +$86M], and the optimizer $134M beyond that. A fixed rule that takes the optimizer's book first matched the model's book (−$14M [−$36M, +$2M]), so the model's value is in the argument — it named 12 of 12 flaws of argument the author wrote into the cases and over-challenged 0 of 12 sound proposals — not in the choice of book. Its failures are in the report as failures: it read a silent tail weight as zero, and 7 of 20 memos written under injected instructions carried a number not on their sheet, which the verifier caught. Section 9 and Figure 9 of the report draw the nine stages and say at each who holds the decision.
